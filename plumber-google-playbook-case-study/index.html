The Plumber Google Couldn't See.

One of the best plumbers in his area — and his whole business ran on word of mouth, because Google couldn't find him. These are the three steps that fixed it, and the month that followed: 151 calls, zero ad spend.

Derek runs a plumbing company, and by every account that matters in the trade, he is good at the work. His customers recommended him. His diary filled through referrals and repeat jobs. But every day, homeowners in his own city searched Google for a plumber — and every day, the same three competitors took the call. He didn't have a skill problem. He had a Google problem.

The starting point: good work, no visibility

Derek's profile existed. Customers could technically find it — several positions below the map's top three, where searchers rarely look. This is the quiet arithmetic of local search: the map shows three businesses first, and those three take the overwhelming share of the calls. Everything below them is close to invisible.

His invisibility had three specific causes. A profile with no proper address anchoring it to the city he served. A website that never said which cities the company worked in. And a review count that lost every comparison against the businesses above him. Each cause had a specific fix — and the fixes had to happen in this order.

1Put your address where the work is

Google's own documentation names three factors behind local rankings: relevance — how well a profile matches the search; distance — how far the business is from the searcher; and prominence — how well known and trusted the business appears to be. Of the three, distance is the one owners most often break without knowing it.

When a homeowner searches "plumber near me," Google measures distance from wherever your profile appears to sit. Derek's profile had no proper address in the city he served, so it started every search in the wrong place. Research firm Whitespark's annual Local Search Ranking Factors survey consistently places proximity at the top of the local-pack factors — estimated at roughly 55% of what decides the top three. A profile pinned in the wrong place fights that weight in every single search.

The fix was simple to describe and strict in its conditions: the profile moved to a real address Derek owned, in a good residential area of the city he actually serves. Real is the operative word. Google verifies business locations, commonly by asking for a video of the premises — and a planted or borrowed address is a suspension risk that can erase a profile entirely. Proximity earned honestly is proximity that survives verification.

The name on the profile follows the same rule. A common piece of advice says to copy the naming pattern of the top three — service, brand word, and city packed into the business name. Google's guidelines say the opposite: the profile name must be the business's real-world name, the one on its paperwork, its signage, its truck. Keyword-stuffed names break the policy, and one competitor report can trigger a suspension. Derek's profile carries his real business name. The ranking work happens in the steps that follow — not in gaming the name.

The rule: real address, real name. If Google can't verify it, don't build on it.

2A page for every service, in every city

The second cause of invisibility sat on Derek's website — or rather, off it. His site never clearly stated which cities the company served. Google cannot rank a business for places it has never mentioned, so entire towns' worth of searches passed him by.

People don't search for "plumber, the whole state." They search for a plumber near them, in their city. A website answers those searches one page at a time: one page for every service, in every city served. For Derek, that meant twenty pages, each one written for its own search.

Twenty pages of genuine, city-specific writing beat any number of duplicated pages with the city name swapped in. Search engines have recognised that pattern for years, and it earns nothing.

The rule: real pages rank. Copy pages don't.

3Close the review gap — slowly

Google cannot judge workmanship. It can only read what customers say — which makes the review count a direct input into prominence, one of Google's three named ranking factors. Derek's profile held 30 reviews. The three businesses above him held far more.

Anyone can run this comparison for their own market in a minute: search the service, note the top three review counts — in Derek's market they sat around 150, 180, and 200 — then look at your own. The difference is the gap, and the gap is the problem stated as a single number. Your job is to close it.

Two disciplines closed it. The first is asking. The best moment is immediately after a finished job, while the customer is looking at work they are happy with. Derek also held years of past customers' names and email addresses — an asset most owners sit on without using. A message or a call to those customers, asking plainly for a review, produced reviews no advertising could have bought.

The second discipline is pace. Reviews that arrive in a sudden burst look manufactured, and profiles get flagged for it. A steady flow — a few a week, the way finished jobs naturally occur — reads as what it is: a working business. It also matches how people actually choose: BrightLocal's consumer research finds that 74% of consumers only pay attention to reviews from the last three months, so a steadily fed profile keeps producing fresh proof while a one-time burst goes stale.

The wording inside the reviews matters as much as the count. A review mentioning a nice pair of socks tells Google nothing. A review saying the water heater was repaired the same day tells Google exactly which service this business performs — and reviews naming the service feed the relevance factor every time someone searches for it.

The result: Derek's reviews climbed from 30 to 70. With the corrected address and the twenty new pages, the profile moved into the top three — and the following month brought 151 calls from Google. No ad spend behind the number.

Sources

The research claims in this breakdown come from named sources:

  • Google Business Profile Help — Tips to improve your local ranking on Google — local results are based mainly on relevance, distance, and prominence; the business name must reflect the real-world name.
  • Whitespark — Local Search Ranking Factors (annual expert survey) — proximity is the largest single local-pack factor, estimated around 55%; reviews are a significant and rising factor.
  • BrightLocal — Local Consumer Review Survey — 74% of consumers only care about reviews from the last three months; 47% will not use a business with fewer than 20 reviews.

A note on what's real

This is one business, in one market, over one period. The three steps are not a formula that prints the same result everywhere — every city's competition is different, and no honest practitioner can promise a call count or a timeline. What can be promised is the check itself: the top three in any city are visibly doing this work, and any owner can verify that tonight, for free, from the search bar. No keyword-stuffed names, no planted addresses, no review bursts — the shortcuts are exactly the things that get profiles suspended.
